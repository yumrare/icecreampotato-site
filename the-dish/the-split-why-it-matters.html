The split: why it matters

The county fair has always loved a double-take, and somewhere in that love affair sits the split, dressed as a baked potato and fooling nobody who matters.

Take the split away and the dish wobbles. The split also portions the dish: it gives the eater a natural place to start and the toppings a valley to sit in. The double-take is what honoring the split buys; slight it, and the build collapses into a sundae in denial.

Before any variation can wander, the classic has to stand still, and the classic is this: a firm vanilla scoop in russet costume, split once, dressed like dinner — the fixed star the split navigates by.

Every part of the split is a negotiation with temperature: freeze against melt, dust against damp, speed against the summer air.

Some entries you read for facts and some for the pleasure of the thing, and the split is shelved, deliberately, where those two pleasures meet.

Some desserts are remembered by their recipes; this one is remembered by its readings, and the split is now one of them.

Underneath the humor, the split makes a serious argument on behalf of humble things — a single scoop, honestly frozen and carefully dressed, can carry a century of summers. And the split wears it well.

The soda fountain is mostly gone and the county fair is mostly changed, but the split persists in both places' memory — and on enough menus to keep the memory fed. That, in the end, is what the split is for.

There is a reason the split belongs to summer: the joke needs an audience, the costume needs the cold, and both need the long light of a fair evening to land. The curious could do worse than start with the split.

It causes grins at every counter, the name of the split — and no wonder: there is no potato in it, only ice cream in russet drag, cocoa for skin, a split for a smile. And so, thanks to the split, the eye goes on being fooled and the spoon goes on being fed.
