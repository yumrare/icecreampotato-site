Workshop note: cleaning the mold

If the fairground had a pantry of legends, cleaning the mold would sit on the shelf between the prize pig and the giant pumpkin — homegrown, unlikely, and entirely beloved.

Cocoa and cream build up in a form's seams and print flaws onto the next skin. Wash, dry fully, and return the form to the freezer — a wet form freezes to the scoop. The mold is the dish's only real tool. It is cared for like one.

The costume only works because the classic underneath is disciplined: a scoop frozen hard enough to hold its oval, and cleaning the mold leans on that discipline in every bite.

Look closely at cleaning the mold and the workmanship shows — in the mottling of the skin, the shoulders of the split, the peak of the cream — the way stitching shows on a good glove.

If you are meeting this dish for the first time through cleaning the mold, welcome to one of dessert's longest-running gags — and one of its most affectionate ones.

However it is dressed, cleaning the mold keeps the promise the dish has always made: first it fools you, then it feeds you, and it never once apologizes for the joke.

Potato country gave the dish its costume, but the fair gave it its audience, and cleaning the mold plays to that audience still — families, skeptics, and one delighted kid at the front. cleaning the mold tells it as a story — no words required.

Old-timers at the fair will tell you cleaning the mold outsells its own punchline — people come for the gag and come back for the eating, summer after summer. And cleaning the mold wears it well.

The straight face is the dish's oldest serving tool, and cleaning the mold is served with it still: no wink, no apology, just a potato that is not one, set down like dinner. Readers tend to remember cleaning the mold long after the fair closes.

The grins at the counter start with the name, so settle it: cleaning the mold holds no potato and never did — the potato is the costume, worn in cocoa and cream. The fair is in cleaning the mold, every time.
